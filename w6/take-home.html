Checking an app for a client

A client asks you for an app, or tells you they already use one. You don't have to like apps. If a client uses one, you can check it. Five checks, in about five minutes:

1. What does it claim?

Find the sentence on the store page that says it works ("proven", "reduces", "clinically", "science"). When was the app last updated? (iPhone: Version History. Android: About this app.)

Stars and reviews tell you who kept using it and liked it, not whether it works.

2. Is there a study, compared with what?

Search the app's name plus "study", or ask an AI chat for a link (type only the app's name, nothing about any client). Open the study yourself. It must be a study of this app. Was there a group that didn't get the app? What did that group get: a waiting list, a fake app, real treatment?

The fairer the comparison, the smaller the effect. "None found" is the most common answer.

3. Will she keep using it?

What is free, and what costs money each month? What does it ask for before you can start?

Most people stop within days. What helps: it feels useful to her, it is tied to the therapy (give it a job, ask about it next session), a person in the loop, and knowing it is practice, not magic.

4. What does it actually do?

Open the main thing it promises and try it for two minutes. Which therapy technique is it, if any? Is that what the store said? Is it easy to use? Then the clinical question: how would this client use it, and what would she learn from it?

5. What happens to her data?

iPhone: App Privacy on the store page (Data Used to Track You, Data Linked to You). Android: Data safety (shared, collected, security practices). Is there a privacy policy you can read before you start? What would she put into it?

Both labels are filled in by the app maker. Google's "sharing" does not count companies that work for the app maker, and neither store checks the form.

The American Psychiatric Association's screener

Its App Evaluation Model opens with eight questions to ask before using an app with a patient. In class you answered five of them:

  • Has it been updated recently (in the last 180 days)? check 1
  • Is there evidence of specific benefit? check 2
  • Does it have a clinical or recovery foundation? check 4
  • Is it easy to use? check 4
  • Is there a clear privacy policy you can read before use? check 5
  • Is sensitive data sent securely? half: Android's label says "encrypted in transit"; the iPhone label doesn't say
  • Which phones and computers does it work on?
  • Can its data be shared with you, the clinician, in a form you can use?

What your association says

The Israel Psychological Association (הפ״י), ethics committee, guidelines on AI tools, 23.3.26:

Section ה: be alert to clients' use of AI tools, including mood apps; ask about them. A psychologist who recommends a tool checks it first, fits it to the client, and keeps track of how it is used and how it affects the treatment.
Section ט: a psychologist whose name is on an AI tool is responsible for its quality and content, for how it collects users' data, and for their privacy.

Summarised in English; the Hebrew text is the authority.
